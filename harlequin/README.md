# Harlequin

[Harlequin Ltd.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harlequin_%28software_company%29)
was a company founded by Jo Marks in Cambridge, UK, 
and later expanded to have interests in the US and Australia.

Its flagship offering was a Postscript Raster Image Processor (RIP),
but it also produced offerings in the languages 
Lisp (LispWorks), ML (MLWorks), and Dylan (DylanWorks).

Harlequin was one of several major players in the Lisp world who helped fund
the writing of the specification document that was later adopted as 
the American National Standard for Common Lisp
(commonly, ANSI Common Lisp or ANSI CL).

It's worth noting, because it might occur to someone later pondering 
the historical antitrust
implications of the arrangement, that 
Harlequin was one of several vendors contributing funding
for that authorship effort, but, importantly, none of those vendors had any
corresponding editorial control. That part was meticulously planned
by some people from Franz Inc, who helpfully thought it all through and
devised a plan to stay ethically clean but do what needed to be done.
All technical decisions about content were
under X3J13, all presentational control was left to me (Kent Pitman) as
Project Editor,
in order that neither X3J13 nor ANSI could claim authorship, but none of the 
funding agencies could be seen as doing inappropriate collusion under antitrust law.
This work began at Symbolics, with a promise that the resulting work would be offered
into the Public Domain, but when Symbolics went bankrupt, Harlequin hired me
and paid for the completion of the work.

Harlequin later funded the development of the
[Common Lisp HyperSpec (CLHS)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_Lisp_HyperSpec),
which put Harlequin's LispWorks product on the visibility map as a major Lisp vendor.
In my subjective opinion, people had previously seen the major Lisp players as either
Lisp Machines (Symbolics in one major fork and LMI, MIT, and TI in the other fork),
or else Franz and Lucid. I sold the idea to Harlequin that if it distributed CLHS
at no cost, it would go everywhere and be a kind of "advertising virus" (my term for
an entity that was something most people would want to obtain, it being at least
cost-free, or "free as in beer" as some say, but would
come with an indelible company logo). They seemed
a little concerned they would have no way to measure who had seen it, but I 
eventually convinced them that this didn't matter if it put them on the map.
It seemed to later fulfill that promise.
More details on this saga are to be had in my conference paper
[Common Lisp: 
 The Untold Story](https://nhplace.com/kent/Papers/cl-untold-story.html)
from the 50th Anniversary of Lisp Conference.

When Lucid, the company that produced Lucid Common Lisp (LCL), was going away,
Harlequin also purchased, maintained, and continued to sell the
Lucid Common Lisp implementation, even at the risk that it would
compete with its own LispWorks product.
The LCL product was later renamed Liquid Common Lisp.
(The company that is today called Lucid is no relation to any of this.)

My sense was that Jo Marks, the company's CEO, just couldn't bear to see LCL
product just disappear. He seemed, in some ways, almost a collector, but in any
case also seemed, again in my personal estimation,
to want to be a good steward of technology and a societal contributor.
I perceived plenty that was controversial in some of his choices from a business
standpoint, but I really had to admire his willingness to stake out ideals
and go after a vision in an honorable way.

Harlequin also purchased and expanded the WebMaker product, a rule-based tool
that converted Adobe FrameMaker's 
[Maker Interchange Format
(MIF)](https://help.adobe.com/en_US/framemaker/2022/pdfs/fm-mif-reference.pdf)
files into HTML.

## Surviving Legacy

Harlequin's influence continues today, decades after the company's demise:

* [Lispworks Ltd.](https://www.lispworks.com/) continues to offer:
  * [Lispworks](https://www.lispworks.com/products/lispworks.html),
    which purports to conform to ANSI CL.
  * [Liquid Common Lisp](https://www.lispworks.com/products/lcl.html)
  * The 
    [Common Lisp HyperSpec](https://www.lispworks.com/documentation/HyperSpec/Front/)

* The Dylan Language is available today as
  [Open Dylan](https://opendylan.org/index.html)

  See also the Dylan documents I've assembled here.

* I believe Harlequin's PostScript RIP also survives, sold by one or more other
  companies today. It was very fast and supported interesting product   
  features like color matching across arrays of inexpensive printers, and 
  optical kerning. But you'll have to do your own research about that. It's not
  my area of expertise. This isn't intended as an up-to-date info page anyway,
  just as a set of clues about the context of the artifacts you might find
  in this repository.
